Examples

Dutch and English look alike. That's the trap.

A word-for-word reading gets surprisingly far, until it doesn't. Here are a few places where context, culture and convention matter more than the dictionary.

False friends

“Eventuele vragen kunt u per e-mail stellen.”

Word for word: Eventual questions can be asked by email.

In context: If you have any questions, feel free to email us.

“Eventueel” means possible or if necessary, never eventual.

False friends

“Bekijk de actuele cijfers.”

Word for word: View the actual figures.

In context: View the latest figures.

“Actueel” means current. “Actual” suggests the other figures were wrong.

Idioms

“Nu komt de aap uit de mouw.”

Word for word: Now the monkey comes out of the sleeve.

In context: So that's what this is really about.

The picture is vivid in Dutch and meaningless in English. The point has to survive, not the monkey.

Idioms

“Onze klanten zijn niet van gisteren.”

Word for word: Our customers are not from yesterday.

In context: Our customers weren't born yesterday.

English has the same idea in a slightly different shape. Finding the match is the job.

Context

“De schepen keurden het plan goed.”

Word for word: The ships approved the plan.

In context: The aldermen approved the plan.

In Flemish local government a “schepen” is an alderman. In a harbour report, it really is a ship.

Official names

“Het wetsvoorstel ligt bij de Tweede Kamer.”

Word for word: The bill is with the Second Chamber.

In context: The bill is before the House of Representatives.

Institutions have established English names. Using the wrong one looks careless to anyone who knows.

Numbers

“De staatsschuld bedraagt 1 biljoen euro.”

Word for word: The national debt amounts to 1 billion euros.

In context: The national debt amounts to €1 trillion.

A Dutch “biljoen” is a million million. One letter, a factor of a thousand.

Dates and formats

“Inschrijven kan tot 09/10, kosten € 1.250,50.”

Copied as is: Register by 09/10, fee € 1.250,50.

In context: Register by 9 October. The fee is €1,250.50.

An American reader sees 10 September and a price with the separators backwards.

Culture

“Het was een gezellige avond.”

Word for word: It was a cosy evening.

In context: We had a lovely evening together.

“Gezellig” can be cosy, fun, sociable or warm, depending on who's talking and where. No single word covers it.
